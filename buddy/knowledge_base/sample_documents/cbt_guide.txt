Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) Guide for Students

What is CBT?
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy is an evidence-based psychological treatment that helps people identify and change negative thinking patterns and behaviors. CBT is one of the most widely researched forms of therapy and has been shown to be effective for depression, anxiety, PTSD, and many other mental health conditions.

Core Principles of CBT:
1. Our thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are interconnected
2. Negative or unrealistic thoughts can trap us in a vicious cycle
3. By changing our thoughts, we can change how we feel and act
4. We can learn skills to manage our mental health independently

Common Cognitive Distortions:
- All-or-Nothing Thinking: Seeing things in black and white with no middle ground. Example: "If I don't get an A, I'm a complete failure."
- Catastrophizing: Expecting the worst possible outcome. Example: "If I fail this exam, my life is over."
- Mind Reading: Assuming you know what others are thinking. Example: "Everyone thinks I'm stupid."
- Overgeneralization: Drawing broad conclusions from a single event. Example: "I failed once, so I'll always fail."
- Should Statements: Rigid rules about how things should be. Example: "I should be able to handle everything perfectly."
- Emotional Reasoning: Believing that because you feel something, it must be true. Example: "I feel worthless, so I must be worthless."

Thought Challenging Technique:
1. Identify the automatic negative thought
2. Examine the evidence for and against this thought
3. Ask: "What would I tell a friend who had this thought?"
4. Create a more balanced, realistic alternative thought
5. Rate how much you believe the new thought

Behavioral Activation:
When feeling depressed, people often withdraw from activities they enjoy. Behavioral activation involves gradually re-engaging with pleasurable and meaningful activities:
- Start small: even a 5-minute walk counts
- Schedule activities in advance
- Track your mood before and after each activity
- Notice which activities improve your mood
- Gradually increase the frequency and duration

Problem-Solving Steps:
1. Define the problem clearly
2. Brainstorm all possible solutions (don't judge yet)
3. Evaluate each solution's pros and cons
4. Choose the best option
5. Create an action plan
6. Review how it went and adjust if needed

When to Seek Professional Help:
- If negative thoughts persist for more than two weeks
- If you're unable to perform daily activities
- If you're having thoughts of self-harm or suicide
- If coping strategies aren't providing relief
- If substance use is increasing
